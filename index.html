Plain-language medical dictionary

Understand the words.

Look up cardiac and electrophysiology terms with simple definitions, detailed explanations, and why each concept matters in learning.

12 terms
HEART ANATOMY

Atrium (plural: atria)

One of the heart’s two upper chambers.

Explain it in more detail

The right atrium receives blood returning from the body. The left atrium receives blood returning from the lungs. Both upper chambers help move blood into the lower chambers, called ventricles.

Why it matters in EP: Electrical activity can begin in or travel through atrial tissue. A surface ECG may show atrial activity as P waves, although real recordings can be more complicated than a teaching diagram.

Remember: Atrium = upper receiving chamber.

HEART ANATOMY

Ventricle

One of the heart’s two lower pumping chambers.

Explain it in more detail

The right ventricle pumps blood toward the lungs. The left ventricle pumps blood out to the body. Their muscular walls contract to push blood forward.

Why it matters in EP: The QRS complex on a typical surface ECG mainly represents the electrical activation of the ventricles. It is an electrical event, not a direct picture of the squeezing motion itself.

Remember: Ventricle = lower pumping chamber.

SIGNALS & RECORDINGS

ECG / EKG

A recording of the heart’s electrical activity from electrodes on the skin.

Explain it in more detail

Electrodes on the skin detect tiny voltage differences produced as heart cells activate and recover. The machine displays those changing differences over time as a tracing. ECG and EKG mean the same thing.

An ECG does not directly show the heart’s anatomy, and a single tracing does not automatically explain why a pattern occurred. Clinicians interpret it alongside symptoms, history, recording quality, and other information.

Remember: An ECG records electrical signals from the body surface.

ECG COMPONENTS

P wave

A wave that often reflects electrical activation spreading through the atria.

Explain it in more detail

In a typical teaching ECG, the P wave is the small rounded deflection before the QRS complex. It represents atrial depolarization—the electrical change that activates atrial muscle.

A P wave may be small, hidden, or hard to identify in some recordings. Not seeing a clear P wave does not, by itself, prove that atrial activity is absent or identify a particular rhythm.

Remember: P wave is commonly associated with atrial electrical activation.

ECG COMPONENTS

QRS complex

The usually sharp group of waves associated with ventricular electrical activation.

Explain it in more detail

The QRS complex reflects depolarization of the ventricles. It often looks taller and narrower than other ECG components because ventricular activation produces a strong combined signal at the skin.

Its shape and width can vary for many reasons. A simplified drawing helps you learn where to look, but real ECG interpretation depends on the full tracing and context.

Remember: QRS = ventricular depolarization.

ECG COMPONENTS

T wave

A wave that usually represents the ventricles’ electrical recovery phase.

Explain it in more detail

After ventricular activation, heart cells undergo electrical recovery, called repolarization. On a typical ECG this is represented by the T wave, which often follows the QRS complex.

The T wave is not the heart muscle “causing” the next QRS. These are different parts of the repeating electrical cycle.

Remember: T wave = ventricular repolarization in a typical ECG.

ELECTRICAL BASICS

Depolarization

An electrical change that activates a heart cell.

Explain it in more detail

Heart cells maintain an electrical difference across their membranes. When a cell is activated, that voltage changes in a rapid, organized way. This process is called depolarization.

When many heart cells depolarize in a coordinated pattern, their combined electrical activity can be detected by recording equipment. Depolarization is electrical activation; it is closely linked to contraction, but it is not the same thing as the mechanical squeeze itself.

Remember: Depolarization = electrical activation.

ELECTRICAL BASICS

Repolarization

The process in which an activated heart cell returns toward its resting electrical state.

Explain it in more detail

After depolarization, the cell’s electrical state changes back toward its resting condition. This recovery process helps the cell be ready for another activation. On a typical surface ECG, ventricular repolarization is represented mainly by the T wave.

Different parts of the heart recover at slightly different times. The real pattern is more complex than a single line or one simple wave.

Remember: Repolarization = electrical recovery.

TIMING & RHYTHM

Rhythm regularity

How evenly spaced repeated heartbeats or QRS complexes appear.

Explain it in more detail

To examine regularity in a tracing, compare the distance from one QRS complex to the next. Similar distances suggest fairly regular timing in that section; changing distances suggest variability.

Regularity is an observation, not a complete diagnosis. Several different rhythms can look regular, and irregular patterns can have different explanations. Always separate what is visible from what you infer.

Remember: Regularity describes spacing, not the cause of the pattern.

TIMING & RHYTHM

R–R interval

The time between one R-wave peak and the next.

Explain it in more detail

The R wave is usually the tallest part of the QRS complex. The R–R interval measures the time between consecutive R-wave peaks. A shorter interval means the peaks are closer together in time; a longer interval means they are farther apart.

On a real ECG, the paper speed or digital time scale matters if you want an actual duration. The website’s schematic traces are illustrations and do not represent measured clinical intervals.

Remember: R–R interval = peak-to-peak timing.

EP SIGNALS

Intracardiac electrogram

An electrical recording made from inside the heart using specialized equipment.

Explain it in more detail

In clinical electrophysiology, specialized catheters with electrodes can record local electrical activity from inside the heart. These recordings are called intracardiac electrograms. They are different from a surface ECG because the electrodes are located inside the heart and detect nearby signals.

Reading them requires training and context. A simplified display can teach the idea of comparing timing, but it cannot reproduce all the information or judgment needed for a real procedure.

Remember: Surface ECG = skin electrodes; intracardiac electrogram = electrodes inside the heart.

CORE CONCEPTS

Electrophysiology (EP)

The study of how electrical signals start and travel through the heart.

Explain it in more detail

Cardiac electrophysiology looks at the heart’s electrical system: how impulses begin, how they move through tissue, and how the timing of different regions relates to one another. The term can describe a scientific field, a specialty, or a clinical EP study, depending on the context.

This website focuses on education. Learning vocabulary and practicing with fictional signals is not the same as being qualified to interpret patient recordings or perform procedures.

Remember: EP = studying the heart’s electrical activity and timing.
