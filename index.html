HEART ANATOMY

Atrium (plural: atria)

One of the heart’s two upper chambers.

Explain it in more detail

The right atrium receives blood returning from the body. The left atrium receives blood returning from the lungs. Both upper chambers help move blood into the lower chambers, called ventricles.

Why it matters in EP: Electrical activity can begin in or travel through atrial tissue. A surface ECG may show atrial activity as P waves, although real recordings can be more complicated than a teaching diagram.

Remember: Atrium = upper receiving chamber.

HEART ANATOMY

Ventricle

One of the heart’s two lower pumping chambers.

Explain it in more detail

The right ventricle pumps blood toward the lungs. The left ventricle pumps blood out to the body. Their muscular walls contract to push blood forward.

Why it matters in EP: The QRS complex on a typical surface ECG mainly represents the electrical activation of the ventricles. It is an electrical event, not a direct picture of the squeezing motion itself.

Remember: Ventricle = lower pumping chamber.

SIGNALS & RECORDINGS

ECG / EKG

A recording of the heart’s electrical activity from electrodes on the skin.

Explain it in more detail

Electrodes on the skin detect tiny voltage differences produced as heart cells activate and recover. The machine displays those changing differences over time as a tracing. ECG and EKG mean the same thing.

An ECG does not directly show the heart’s anatomy, and a single tracing does not automatically explain why a pattern occurred. Clinicians interpret it alongside symptoms, history, recording quality, and other information.

Remember: An ECG records electrical signals from the body surface.

ECG COMPONENTS

P wave

A wave that often reflects electrical activation spreading through the atria.

Explain it in more detail

In a typical teaching ECG, the P wave is the small rounded deflection before the QRS complex. It represents atrial depolarization—the electrical change that activates atrial muscle.

A P wave may be small, hidden, or hard to identify in some recordings. Not seeing a clear P wave does not, by itself, prove that atrial activity is absent or identify a particular rhythm.

Remember: P wave is commonly associated with atrial electrical activation.

ECG COMPONENTS

QRS complex

The usually sharp group of waves associated with ventricular electrical activation.

Explain it in more detail

The QRS complex reflects depolarization of the ventricles. It often looks taller and narrower than other ECG components because ventricular activation produces a strong combined signal at the skin.

Its shape and width can vary for many reasons. A simplified drawing helps you learn where to look, but real ECG interpretation depends on the full tracing and context.

Remember: QRS = ventricular depolarization.

ECG COMPONENTS

T wave

A wave that usually represents the ventricles’ electrical recovery phase.

Explain it in more detail

After ventricular activation, heart cells undergo electrical recovery, called repolarization. On a typical ECG this is represented by the T wave, which often follows the QRS complex.

The T wave is not the heart muscle “causing” the next QRS. These are different parts of the repeating electrical cycle.

Remember: T wave = ventricular repolarization in a typical ECG.

ELECTRICAL BASICS

Depolarization

An electrical change that activates a heart cell.

Explain it in more detail

Heart cells maintain an electrical difference across their membranes. When a cell is activated, that voltage changes in a rapid, organized way. This process is called depolarization.

When many heart cells depolarize in a coordinated pattern, their combined electrical activity can be detected by recording equipment. Depolarization is electrical activation; it is closely linked to contraction, but it is not the same thing as the mechanical squeeze itself.

Remember: Depolarization = electrical activation.

ELECTRICAL BASICS

Repolarization

The process in which an activated heart cell returns toward its resting electrical state.

Explain it in more detail

After depolarization, the cell’s electrical state changes back toward its resting condition. This recovery process helps the cell be ready for another activation. On a typical surface ECG, ventricular repolarization is represented mainly by the T wave.

Different parts of the heart recover at slightly different times. The real pattern is more complex than a single line or one simple wave.

Remember: Repolarization = electrical recovery.

TIMING & RHYTHM

Rhythm regularity

How evenly spaced repeated heartbeats or QRS complexes appear.

Explain it in more detail

To examine regularity in a tracing, compare the distance from one QRS complex to the next. Similar distances suggest fairly regular timing in that section; changing distances suggest variability.

Regularity is an observation, not a complete diagnosis. Several different rhythms can look regular, and irregular patterns can have different explanations. Always separate what is visible from what you infer.

Remember: Regularity describes spacing, not the cause of the pattern.

TIMING & RHYTHM

R–R interval

The time between one R-wave peak and the next.

Explain it in more detail

The R wave is usually the tallest part of the QRS complex. The R–R interval measures the time between consecutive R-wave peaks. A shorter interval means the peaks are closer together in time; a longer interval means they are farther apart.

On a real ECG, the paper speed or digital time scale matters if you want an actual duration. The website’s schematic traces are illustrations and do not represent measured clinical intervals.

Remember: R–R interval = peak-to-peak timing.

EP SIGNALS

Intracardiac electrogram

An electrical recording made from inside the heart using specialized equipment.

Explain it in more detail

In clinical electrophysiology, specialized catheters with electrodes can record local electrical activity from inside the heart. These recordings are called intracardiac electrograms. They are different from a surface ECG because the electrodes are located inside the heart and detect nearby signals.

Reading them requires training and context. A simplified display can teach the idea of comparing timing, but it cannot reproduce all the information or judgment needed for a real procedure.

Remember: Surface ECG = skin electrodes; intracardiac electrogram = electrodes inside the heart.

CORE CONCEPTS

Electrophysiology (EP)

The study of how electrical signals start and travel through the heart.

Explain it in more detail

Cardiac electrophysiology looks at the heart’s electrical system: how impulses begin, how they move through tissue, and how the timing of different regions relates to one another. The term can describe a scientific field, a specialty, or a clinical EP study, depending on the context.

This website focuses on education. Learning vocabulary and practicing with fictional signals is not the same as being qualified to interpret patient recordings or perform procedures.

Remember: EP = studying the heart’s electrical activity and timing.

HEART ANATOMY

Right atrium

The heart chamber that receives blood returning from the body.

Explain it in more detail

It receives blood through the venae cavae and passes it toward the right ventricle through the tricuspid valve. In EP, right-atrial recordings can help show atrial activation timing.

Remember: Think: right atrium receives body-returning blood.

HEART ANATOMY

Left atrium

The upper-left heart chamber that receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs.

Explain it in more detail

It passes blood to the left ventricle. Electrical activity in atrial tissue contributes to the P wave on a surface ECG.

Remember: Think: left atrium receives blood from the lungs.

CONDUCTION SYSTEM

SA node

The sinoatrial node, the heart’s usual natural pacemaker.

Explain it in more detail

Specialized cells in the right atrium normally initiate each heartbeat. Its activity influences sinus rhythm and atrial activation.

Remember: SA node = usual starting point.

CONDUCTION SYSTEM

AV node

The atrioventricular node, which conducts impulses from atria toward ventricles with a delay.

Explain it in more detail

The delay allows time for ventricular filling. AV nodal conduction is important when learning PR intervals and some supraventricular tachycardias.

Remember: AV node = gateway with a delay.

CONDUCTION SYSTEM

His bundle

A bundle of specialized conducting tissue carrying impulses from the AV node into the ventricular conduction system.

Explain it in more detail

It divides into right and left bundle branches. Recordings near the His bundle can help characterize conduction during an EP study.

Remember: His signal helps assess conduction toward the ventricles.

CONDUCTION SYSTEM

Bundle branches

The right and left pathways that carry electrical activation down the interventricular septum.

Explain it in more detail

Bundle-branch conduction helps rapidly distribute activation through the ventricles. Abnormal conduction can alter QRS shape and duration.

Remember: Branches distribute the impulse to the ventricles.

CONDUCTION SYSTEM

Purkinje fibers

Specialized fibers that rapidly spread electrical activation through ventricular muscle.

Explain it in more detail

They help coordinate ventricular contraction. Their role matters in understanding ventricular activation and certain arrhythmias.

Remember: Purkinje fibers spread activation quickly.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

PR interval

The time from the start of the P wave to the start of the QRS complex.

Explain it in more detail

It includes atrial depolarization and conduction through the AV junction and His-Purkinje system. Interpretations depend on the ECG context.

Remember: PR = atrial start to ventricular activation start.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

QRS duration

The width of the QRS complex, reflecting the time taken for ventricular depolarization on a surface ECG.

Explain it in more detail

A wider QRS may reflect altered ventricular conduction or a ventricular-origin rhythm, but it must be interpreted with the whole tracing and clinical context.

Remember: QRS duration describes ventricular activation time.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

QT interval

The interval from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave.

Explain it in more detail

It represents ventricular depolarization and repolarization together. QT varies with heart rate; corrected QT estimates account for rate.

Remember: QT covers ventricular activation and recovery.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

ST segment

The portion between the end of the QRS complex and the start of the T wave.

Explain it in more detail

It is commonly assessed in ECG interpretation. In this learning site, it is introduced as waveform anatomy rather than a diagnostic rule.

Remember: ST segment follows the QRS.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

Sinus rhythm

A rhythm in which activation normally begins in the sinoatrial node.

Explain it in more detail

A typical teaching ECG shows a consistent P wave before each QRS, but real tracings require careful interpretation.

Remember: Sinus rhythm begins at the SA node.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

Heart rate

The number of heartbeats per minute.

Explain it in more detail

Rate can be estimated from ECG timing. Rate alone does not identify the rhythm or explain its cause.

Remember: Rate tells how fast, not necessarily what rhythm.

ECG FUNDAMENTALS

Artifact

A signal distortion caused by movement, electrical interference, or recording issues rather than cardiac activity.

Explain it in more detail

Artifact can mimic or obscure ECG and electrogram features. Check signal quality before interpreting a waveform.

Remember: Artifact can look like a signal but may not be cardiac.

EP STUDY BASICS

Electrophysiology study

A procedure that records and evaluates electrical activity in the heart using specialized catheters.

Explain it in more detail

An EP study can assess conduction and investigate arrhythmia mechanisms. This dictionary is educational and does not provide clinical instruction.

Remember: EP study = specialized recording and testing of heart electricity.

EP STUDY BASICS

Electrode

A conductive contact used to sense or deliver electrical signals.

Explain it in more detail

Electrodes on the skin record surface ECGs; catheter electrodes can record intracardiac signals or deliver pacing stimuli.

Remember: Electrodes sense or deliver electrical activity.

EP STUDY BASICS

Catheter

A thin flexible tube that can carry electrodes into the heart during an EP procedure.

Explain it in more detail

Different catheters have different electrode arrangements and recording positions. Placement and use require trained clinical professionals.

Remember: A catheter can carry recording electrodes.

EP STUDY BASICS

Pacing

Delivering timed electrical stimuli to test or influence cardiac activation.

Explain it in more detail

In EP education, pacing helps illustrate conduction, refractoriness, and how activation sequences change. Actual procedures are performed by qualified teams.

Remember: Pacing uses timed stimuli.

EP STUDY BASICS

Pacing stimulus

A controlled electrical pulse delivered through an electrode.

Explain it in more detail

A stimulus may be followed by a captured cardiac response if tissue is activated. The stimulus artifact itself is distinct from the response.

Remember: Stimulus first; response if capture occurs.

EP STUDY BASICS

Capture

Activation of cardiac tissue by a pacing stimulus.

Explain it in more detail

Capture means the stimulus successfully depolarized the intended tissue. It is different from simply seeing a stimulus artifact on a recording.

Remember: Capture = tissue responded to the stimulus.

EP STUDY BASICS

Refractory period

A period when cardiac tissue cannot be activated normally, or requires a stronger stimulus to respond.

Explain it in more detail

Refractoriness changes during the cardiac cycle and is important to conduction and arrhythmia mechanisms.

Remember: Refractory tissue temporarily resists activation.

EP STUDY BASICS

Conduction

The spread of an electrical impulse through cardiac tissue.

Explain it in more detail

Conduction speed and pathways affect timing between atrial, His, and ventricular signals.

Remember: Conduction = travel of an impulse.

EP STUDY BASICS

Conduction delay

Slower-than-expected propagation of an electrical impulse through a pathway.

Explain it in more detail

A delay may appear as a longer interval between recorded events. Its meaning depends on where signals were recorded and the broader study.

Remember: A delay changes the timing between signals.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

Intracardiac electrogram

A recording of electrical activity from inside the heart, usually via catheter electrodes.

Explain it in more detail

Intracardiac electrograms can show local activation timing that may not be obvious on a surface ECG. Signal labels depend on recording location.

Remember: An electrogram records local cardiac electrical activity.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

Atrial electrogram

An intracardiac recording of electrical activity from atrial tissue.

Explain it in more detail

Its timing can be compared with surface P waves and other intracardiac signals in simplified EP examples.

Remember: Atrial electrogram = recorded atrial activity.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

Ventricular electrogram

An intracardiac recording of electrical activity from ventricular tissue.

Explain it in more detail

It can be compared with the surface QRS and other intracardiac events to study activation timing.

Remember: Ventricular electrogram = recorded ventricular activity.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

His potential

A recorded electrical signal associated with activation of the His bundle.

Explain it in more detail

The His potential is used in EP timing measurements, including the interval between His activation and ventricular activation.

Remember: His potential marks His-bundle activation.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

A-H interval

The time between atrial activation and His-bundle activation in an EP recording.

Explain it in more detail

It is commonly used to describe conduction through the AV nodal region, interpreted with the recording setup and other measurements.

Remember: A-H = atrium to His.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

H-V interval

The time between His-bundle activation and ventricular activation.

Explain it in more detail

It reflects conduction from the His bundle through the distal conduction system toward ventricular activation.

Remember: H-V = His to ventricle.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

Activation sequence

The order in which different recorded sites become electrically activated.

Explain it in more detail

Comparing sequences can help learners reason about where activation may be traveling from, using simplified examples rather than diagnostic conclusions.

Remember: Sequence = which site activates first, next, and last.

INTRACARDIAC SIGNALS

Local activation time

The measured timing of electrical activation at a particular recording site.

Explain it in more detail

Researchers and clinicians compare local activation times across sites during mapping. Measurements depend on signal quality and consistent annotation.

Remember: Local activation time belongs to one recording location.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Arrhythmia

A heart rhythm that differs from the normal expected rhythm.

Explain it in more detail

Arrhythmias vary widely in mechanism and significance. This site teaches foundational vocabulary and does not diagnose a person’s rhythm.

Remember: Arrhythmia = an abnormal or unusual rhythm.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Tachycardia

A faster-than-usual heart rate.

Explain it in more detail

Tachycardia describes rate, not a single mechanism. It may arise from different cardiac or non-cardiac causes.

Remember: Tachycardia means fast rate.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Bradycardia

A slower-than-usual heart rate.

Explain it in more detail

Bradycardia describes rate and must be interpreted in context, including the person and circumstances.

Remember: Bradycardia means slow rate.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)

A group of fast rhythms that arise from or involve structures above the ventricles.

Explain it in more detail

SVT is an umbrella term covering multiple mechanisms. The exact rhythm cannot be determined from the term alone.

Remember: SVT describes a family of rhythms, not just one.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Atrial fibrillation

An arrhythmia with disorganized atrial electrical activity and an often irregular ventricular response.

Explain it in more detail

It is introduced here as a rhythm concept. Diagnosis and management require clinical assessment and appropriate ECG interpretation.

Remember: Atrial fibrillation involves disorganized atrial activity.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Atrial flutter

An atrial rhythm commonly associated with rapid, organized re-entrant activation.

Explain it in more detail

A surface ECG may show repetitive atrial activity, but appearances vary. This entry explains terminology rather than diagnosis.

Remember: Flutter is typically more organized than fibrillation.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Ventricular tachycardia (VT)

A fast rhythm originating in the ventricles.

Explain it in more detail

VT has important clinical implications and requires professional assessment. Here it is presented only as a foundational EP term.

Remember: VT begins in ventricular tissue.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Re-entry

A mechanism in which an electrical impulse repeatedly travels around a circuit of excitable tissue.

Explain it in more detail

Re-entry can sustain some tachycardias. It depends on conduction pathways and tissue recovery, and is often taught using circuit diagrams.

Remember: Re-entry = an impulse circulates and reactivates tissue.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Automaticity

The ability of certain cardiac cells to spontaneously generate electrical impulses.

Explain it in more detail

Normal pacemaker activity depends on automaticity; changes in it can contribute to rhythm disturbances.

Remember: Automaticity = ability to start an impulse spontaneously.

ARRHYTHMIA CONCEPTS

Ectopic beat

A heartbeat initiated from a site other than the usual dominant pacemaker.

Explain it in more detail

Ectopic beats may arise from atrial, junctional, or ventricular tissue. Their significance depends on context and pattern.

Remember: Ectopic = starting from another location.

MAPPING & ABLATION

Electroanatomical mapping

A method of combining electrical measurements with a three-dimensional representation of cardiac anatomy.

Explain it in more detail

Mapping systems can help represent activation or voltage patterns. The details are more advanced than this introductory definition.

Remember: Mapping combines electrical information with anatomy.

MAPPING & ABLATION

Ablation

A procedure that creates targeted lesions to modify tissue involved in an arrhythmia.

Explain it in more detail

Ablation is performed by trained clinical teams after appropriate assessment. This entry is educational and not procedural guidance.

Remember: Ablation targets tissue to change an electrical pathway.

MAPPING & ABLATION

Radiofrequency energy

A form of energy used in some ablation procedures to heat tissue.

Explain it in more detail

It is one of several energy approaches used in clinical electrophysiology. Selection and application are clinical decisions.

Remember: Radiofrequency ablation uses controlled heating.

MAPPING & ABLATION

Cryoablation

An ablation approach that uses cooling to affect targeted tissue.

Explain it in more detail

It is used in selected clinical contexts. The choice of technique depends on the condition, anatomy, evidence, and clinician judgment.

Remember: Cryoablation uses cold rather than heat.

MAPPING & ABLATION

Pulmonary veins

Vessels that carry oxygen-rich blood from the lungs to the left atrium.

Explain it in more detail

The tissue around pulmonary vein openings is important in many discussions of atrial fibrillation and catheter ablation.

Remember: Pulmonary veins connect the lungs to the left atrium.
